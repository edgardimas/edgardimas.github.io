How we give instruction to the machine ▶

First let me introduced you to punch cards. Punch cards were one of the earliest ways people communicated instructions to computers. Punch cards were one of the earliest ways people communicated instructions to computers. Imagine a stiff paper card, roughly the size of a dollar bill, with rows of numbered positions across it. A machine would punch small rectangular holes into specific spots on that card. Each position on the card had just two possible states: either there was a hole, or there wasn't. That's it. A hole meant "1" and no hole meant "0". Workers would prepare stacks of these cards, each one representing a line of instruction, then feed the entire stack into a computer that would read them one by one.

This simple on/off idea, hole or no hole, is the same foundation that computers still use today. Everything you see on a screen, every photo, song, or message, is stored and processed as long chains of 1s and 0s. A punch card just made that idea physical and visible. You could literally hold a "1" or a "0" in your hand. Modern computers work billions of times faster and store information on tiny chips rather than paper, but the core logic hasn't changed: every piece of information comes down to a choice between two states, on or off, yes or no, 1 or 0.

Silicon is not fabricated from scratch but rather refined from its natural form—silica, which is abundant in sand and quartz. The process begins by mixing silica with carbon in an electric arc furnace. At temperatures exceeding 2,000°C, the carbon acts as a reducing agent, stripping away oxygen from the silica and yielding a crude form of silicon known as metallurgical-grade silicon. This initial product contains impurities and is subsequently purified through methods such as the Siemens process or chemical vapor deposition, especially when ultra-high purity is required for semiconductor applications. Thus, through a combination of high-temperature reduction and sophisticated refining techniques, silicon is transformed from an intractable, naturally occurring mineral into a vital component for modern electronics and solar technology.
