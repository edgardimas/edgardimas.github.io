How we give instruction to the machine ▶

When a stack of punch cards was fed into a card reader, the machine would pass each card under a row of small sensors.When a stack of punch cards was fed into a card reader, the machine would pass each card under a row of small sensors. In early readers, these sensors were tiny metal pins or wire brushes. As the card moved through, the pins would either pass through a hole and complete an electrical circuit (registering a "1"), or they would press against solid paper and the circuit stayed open (registering a "0"). The machine read each card line by line, position by position, translating the physical pattern of holes into a stream of electrical signals. Those signals were then passed along to the computer's processor as a sequence of 1s and 0s.

This simple on/off idea, hole or no hole, is the same foundation that computers still use today. Everything you see on a screen, every photo, song, or message, is stored and processed as long chains of 1s and 0s. A punch card just made that idea physical and visible. You could literally hold a "1" or a "0" in your hand. Modern computers work billions of times faster and store information on tiny chips rather than paper, but the core logic hasn't changed: every piece of information comes down to a choice between two states, on or off, yes or no, 1 or 0.

How The machine execute our code ▶

In early readers, these sensors were tiny metal pins or wire brushes. As the card moved through, the pins would either pass through a hole and complete an electrical circuit (registering a "1"), or they would press against solid paper and the circuit stayed open (registering a "0"). The machine read each card line by line, position by position, translating the physical pattern of holes into a stream of electrical signals. Those signals were then passed along to the computer's processor as a sequence of 1s and 0s.

Once the processor received that stream of signals, it matched the patterns against a set of built-in instructions it already knew. For example, a specific pattern of 1s and 0s might mean "add these two numbers" or "store this value in memory." The processor would carry out each instruction in order, one card at a time, working through the whole stack from top to bottom. This is why dropping a stack of punch cards was such a disaster, because the order mattered completely. If the sequence got shuffled, the computer would receive instructions in the wrong order and produce nonsense results or crash entirely. The cards were, in a very literal sense, a physical program.
