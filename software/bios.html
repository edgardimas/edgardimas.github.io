BIOS/UEFI

Introduction

BIOS (Basic Input Output System) is a small program stored on a tiny chip on your computer's motherboard, and it is the very first thing that runs when you press the power button, before any operating system loads. Its job is to wake up and check all the basic hardware components, the processor, memory, keyboard, storage drives, and make sure they are present and responding. This process is called POST, which stands for Power-On Self Test. If everything checks out, the BIOS then looks for an operating system to hand control over to, typically finding it on your hard drive or SSD. Think of it as the building's security guard who checks that all the doors and lights work before letting anyone into the office.

UEFI (Unified Extensible Firmware Interface) is the modern replacement for BIOS, and most computers made in the last decade use it. It does the same fundamental job but with significant improvements. UEFI can handle much larger storage drives, boots faster, has a proper graphical interface with mouse support instead of the old text-only menus, and has better security features like Secure Boot, which checks that the operating system hasn't been tampered with before loading it. The core idea is still the same, a small, permanent program that sits between the raw hardware and the operating system, acting as the first layer of software your machine runs. Without it, your computer would power on but have no idea what to do next, like waking up with no memory of where you are or what you were supposed to do.

Silicon is not fabricated from scratch but rather refined from its natural form—silica, which is abundant in sand and quartz. The process begins by mixing silica with carbon in an electric arc furnace. At temperatures exceeding 2,000°C, the carbon acts as a reducing agent, stripping away oxygen from the silica and yielding a crude form of silicon known as metallurgical-grade silicon. This initial product contains impurities and is subsequently purified through methods such as the Siemens process or chemical vapor deposition, especially when ultra-high purity is required for semiconductor applications. Thus, through a combination of high-temperature reduction and sophisticated refining techniques, silicon is transformed from an intractable, naturally occurring mineral into a vital component for modern electronics and solar technology.
