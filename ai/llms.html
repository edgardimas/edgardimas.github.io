LLMs: Predicting the Next Word

Introduction

A large language model, or LLM, is a neural network trained on a massive amount of text with one core skill at its heart: guessing which word is likely to come next in a sentence. That sounds far too simple to explain something like ChatGPT, but the trick is scale. After reading enormous amounts of text from books, websites, and articles, predicting the next word well enough actually requires the model to pick up grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and writing style along the way, since all of that helps make better predictions.

When you chat with an LLM, it is not looking up a stored answer in a database somewhere. It is generating a response one word, or more precisely one token, at a time, each time asking "given everything so far, what is the most likely next piece of text." It strings these predictions together, one after another, until it produces a full sentence, paragraph, or answer. This is also why LLMs can sometimes state confident-sounding but incorrect information, a problem often called hallucination, since the model is optimizing for plausible-sounding text, not for looking facts up in a verified source.

What a "token" actually is ▶

LLMs do not process text one full word at a time, they break text into smaller chunks called tokens, which might be a whole word, part of a word, or even a single punctuation mark. The word "networking," for example, might get split into pieces like "network" and "ing." This chunking lets the model handle rare words, typos, and made-up words by combining smaller familiar pieces, instead of needing every possible whole word memorized in advance. When you hear about a model's "context window," that number is usually measured in tokens, not plain words.

The transformer, the architecture behind most LLMs ▶

Most modern LLMs are built using a design called the transformer, introduced in a 2017 research paper. Its key idea is a mechanism called attention, which lets the model weigh how relevant every other word in a sentence is when trying to understand or predict one particular word. This is what lets a model correctly connect the word "it" in a long sentence back to the actual noun it refers to, even many words earlier. Before transformers, older architectures struggled to track long-distance relationships like this, which is a big reason transformers took over the field so quickly.
