Prompting: Talking to the Model

Introduction

A prompt is simply the text you type in to ask an AI model for something, and it turns out that how you phrase that text can change the quality of the answer a lot. This is because the model is not reading your mind, it is only reacting to the exact words in front of it, similar to how asking a classmate a vague question like "help with math" gets a worse answer than asking "can you explain how to factor a quadratic equation." The clearer and more specific the request, the more the model has to work with when generating its response.

Good prompting usually means giving context the model would not otherwise have, being specific about the format you want, like a list versus a paragraph, and breaking a big task into smaller steps if it is complicated. Some people call this "prompt engineering," which sounds fancier than it usually is in practice. It is less about secret magic phrases and more about communicating clearly, the same skill that makes you a better writer of emails or instructions to another human, just aimed at a model instead.

Why examples in a prompt help so much ▶

If you show a model one or two examples of exactly the kind of output you want before asking your real question, a technique called few-shot prompting, it often performs noticeably better than being asked cold. This works because the model is fundamentally a pattern predictor, so giving it a pattern to continue plays directly to its core strength. It is similar to showing a new employee one correctly filled-out form before asking them to fill out the next one themselves, rather than only giving them a paragraph of written instructions and hoping for the best.

The model does not actually "know" what it does not know ▶

Because an LLM generates answers by predicting plausible next words rather than checking a database of verified facts, it can sound equally confident whether it is right or completely wrong. This is why it matters to double-check important facts, dates, or numbers an AI gives you, especially for anything with real consequences, the same way you would fact-check a claim from a random person online rather than trusting it purely because it was said confidently. Treating AI output as a strong first draft rather than a guaranteed final answer is a healthy habit.
