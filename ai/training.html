Training: Turning Data Into a Model

Introduction

Training is the actual process of turning a random, useless network into a working model, and it is usually the most expensive and time-consuming part of building AI. It starts with gathering and cleaning a dataset, which can mean anything from millions of labeled photos to a huge scrape of text from books and websites. Messy or biased data at this stage causes problems that show up later, which is why data cleaning, while unglamorous, is one of the most important jobs in the whole process.

Once the data is ready, training happens in repeated rounds called epochs, where the model looks at examples, makes predictions, measures how wrong those predictions were, and adjusts its internal weights slightly using a method called backpropagation. This whole cycle can run for days or even months on powerful computer clusters, especially for today's largest models. Once training finishes, the model's weights are essentially frozen into a final version, sometimes called a checkpoint, which can then be used to make predictions on brand new data it has never seen.

Training data vs test data ▶

If you only ever quiz yourself on questions you already memorized the answers to, you cannot tell whether you actually understand a subject or just memorized it. Machine learning has the same issue, so engineers split their data into a training set, which the model actually learns from, and a separate test set, which the model never sees during training. After training finishes, running the model on that untouched test set reveals whether it genuinely learned useful patterns or just memorized its training examples, a problem called overfitting.

Why training big models costs so much ▶

Training a large modern model can involve running specialized chips around the clock for weeks, consuming huge amounts of electricity and costing companies tens of millions of dollars for a single model. This is very different from using the model afterward, called inference, which is far cheaper and is what actually happens when you type a message and get a quick reply. The heavy, expensive lifting happens once, upfront, during training, and the resulting model can then be reused to answer millions of requests relatively cheaply after that.
