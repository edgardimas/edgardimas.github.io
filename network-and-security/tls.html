TLS / HTTPS: Locking the Conversation

Introduction

Plain HTTP sends everything, including passwords, in a form that anyone watching the network traffic could read, like sending a postcard instead of a sealed letter. HTTPS fixes this by wrapping HTTP inside TLS, or Transport Layer Security, which encrypts the conversation so that only your device and the server can actually read what is being sent. Even if someone on the same public Wi-Fi captured every packet you sent, encrypted traffic would look like meaningless scrambled noise to them.

Before any encrypted data flows, your browser and the server do a quick negotiation called a TLS handshake, where they agree on encryption methods and exchange a secret key that only the two of them know. The server also proves its identity using a digital certificate, similar to showing an ID card, so your browser can confirm it is really talking to the real example.com and not an imposter pretending to be it. That little padlock icon in your browser's address bar is simply telling you this whole handshake succeeded.

What a certificate authority actually does ▶

Anyone could generate a certificate claiming to be example.com, so certificates only mean something because a trusted third party, a certificate authority, digitally signs them after verifying the website owner actually controls that domain. Your browser ships with a built-in list of certificate authorities it trusts, similar to a list of notaries whose signature it will accept. When a server presents a certificate signed by one of those trusted authorities, the browser accepts it as genuine. If a certificate is missing, expired, or signed by nobody your browser trusts, you get the scary warning page.

Why the padlock matters, and what it does not promise ▶

The padlock only guarantees that your connection to that specific server is encrypted and that the server proved its identity through a valid certificate. It says nothing about whether the website itself is trustworthy, safe, or legitimate in what it does with your data. A scam website can absolutely have a valid certificate and a padlock, since certificates only prove "this really is example.com," not "this website is honest." Treat the padlock as proof your traffic cannot be easily eavesdropped on in transit, not as a general seal of approval.
