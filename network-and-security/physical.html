Physical: Actually Moving the Signal

Introduction

Every message on the internet eventually has to become something physical, because computers cannot beam thoughts to each other. Over an ethernet cable, that means tiny changes in electrical voltage. Over fiber optic cable, it means pulses of light flashing on and off. Over Wi-Fi, it means changes in a radio wave. The Physical layer is just this: the actual, real-world way that a 1 or a 0 gets turned into something that can travel from one device to another.

It helps to think of it like Morse code with a flashlight. The idea being sent (dot, dot, dash) is separate from the tool sending it (the flashlight). You could send the same Morse code with a mirror, a foghorn, or a flag, and the message would not change even though the physical method did. Networking works the same way: the same 1s and 0s can travel over copper, glass fiber, or radio waves, and every layer above the physical one does not care which method was used, as long as the bits arrive.

Cables: copper vs fiber ▶

Copper cables, like the Cat5e or Cat6 cable that might run from your router to a game console, send data as electrical voltage differences. They are cheap and easy to install but lose signal strength over long distances and can pick up interference from nearby electronics. Fiber optic cables send data as pulses of light through a thin strand of glass instead. Light does not degrade or pick up electrical interference the way voltage does, so fiber can carry data much farther and much faster, which is why it is used for internet backbones that cross cities, countries, and oceans.

How Wi-Fi turns bits into radio waves ▶

Wi-Fi works by having your router's antenna vibrate an electrical current at a specific radio frequency, commonly around 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz. The device slightly shifts the wave's timing, height, or phase in patterns that represent your data, a trick called modulation. Your laptop's antenna picks up that vibration in the air and reverses the process to pull the original bits back out. Because it is broadcast through open air instead of a wire, Wi-Fi is more exposed to interference from walls, microwaves, and other networks, which is part of why a wired connection is usually more stable.
