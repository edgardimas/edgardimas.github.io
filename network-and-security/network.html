Network: Getting Across the Internet

Introduction

MAC addresses only make sense to devices on the same local network, so once your data needs to leave your house and travel across the internet, it needs a different kind of address: an IP address. Think of a MAC address like a person's name, useful inside one building, and an IP address like a full street address, useful anywhere in the world. The Network layer's whole job is figuring out the best path to get a piece of data from your IP address to the destination's IP address, possibly hopping through dozens of routers along the way.

Each router along the path looks at the destination IP address and makes a quick decision: which cable or connection should this packet go out on next to get closer to its destination? This is called routing, and it happens over and over, router after router, similar to asking for directions at each intersection instead of knowing the entire route in advance. None of those routers need to know the full journey, they just need to know the next best step, which is what makes the internet able to keep working even when some connections go down.

IPv4 vs IPv6 ▶

IPv4 addresses look like 192.168.1.1 and only allow about 4.3 billion unique addresses. That sounds like a lot until you remember phones, laptops, smart TVs, doorbells, and game consoles all need one. IPv6 was created to fix this shortage, using a much longer address format like 2001:0db8:85a3::8a2e:0370:7334, which allows for an almost unimaginably larger number of addresses. Most home networks still rely heavily on IPv4 today, often stretched further with a trick called NAT, but IPv6 adoption keeps growing because the world is genuinely running low on IPv4 addresses to hand out.

NAT: sharing one address for a whole house ▶

Your home probably has one public IP address assigned by your internet provider, yet every device in the house can go online at once. This works through Network Address Translation, or NAT, which your router handles automatically. Your router hands each device a private local IP address, then repackages outgoing traffic to look like it is all coming from the router's single public IP, keeping track of which reply belongs to which device. It is similar to an office building where everyone shares one street address, but the receptionist knows exactly which desk each piece of mail belongs to.
