Intro: What Even Is a Network

Introduction

Imagine you and your friend want to pass notes in class, but you sit on opposite sides of the room. You cannot just shout it, so you pass the note through five other classmates. For that to work, everyone needs to agree on some basic rules: which direction to pass it, how to know who it is for, and what to do if the note gets dropped. A computer network is the exact same idea, except the "classmates" are cables, Wi-Fi signals, and routers, and the "note" is broken into tiny pieces of data.

When you open a website, your phone or laptop is not talking to the website directly in one single conversation. Your request gets wrapped, passed along, unwrapped, and rewrapped multiple times as it hops from your device, through your Wi-Fi router, out to your internet provider, across the internet, and finally into the server that owns the website. Then the whole thing happens again in reverse to bring the answer back to you. This layered hand-off system is usually explained using a model with names like Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, and Application. Each layer only worries about its own job, which is why the whole system does not collapse into chaos.

OSI vs TCP/IP, why two models ▶

You will run into two different lists of layers when you read about networking. The OSI model has seven layers and was designed as a teaching tool to describe every possible job a network needs to do. The TCP/IP model, which is what the actual internet runs on, squishes some of those jobs together into four or five layers. Nobody built a real network exactly like OSI describes it. Think of OSI as the textbook diagram and TCP/IP as the version that engineers actually use, sharing the same basic idea of stacking responsibilities on top of each other.

Why break the job into layers at all ▶

Splitting the work into layers means each part of the system can be built, fixed, or replaced without breaking everything else. Your Wi-Fi card does not need to know anything about websites, and a website does not need to know whether you connected over Wi-Fi, a cable, or your phone's mobile data. Engineers who build routers only worry about moving data efficiently, while engineers who build apps only worry about what the data means. This separation is why the internet can keep growing new technology at the bottom, like faster Wi-Fi standards, without every website needing to be rewritten.
